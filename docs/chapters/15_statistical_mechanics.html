Sticking point 01

I expect the lowest-energy state to have the largest population.

Starting question “Can a higher-energy group of states be more populated?”

A more useful question

Consider a toy model with two groups: energy 0 and degeneracy 1, and energy 2*k_B*T and degeneracy 10. Calculate their canonical population ratio and explain the role of degeneracy.

Why this helps: It states the statistical model and includes the missing count of accessible states.

Compare with an example answer

The higher-to-lower group population ratio is (10/1)*exp(-2), about 1.35. The higher-energy group therefore has about 57.5% of the total population in this two-group model. Its greater degeneracy outweighs its energy penalty. This is a toy calculation, not a simulated molecular population.

Course-authored teaching example. Your AI response may differ.

Follow-up to ask

What happens to the ratio if the higher group's degeneracy is reduced to 1?

Check your understanding

Is a lower energy alone sufficient to rank group populations?

Reveal the check answer

No. Statistical weights include the number of accessible states.

Sticking point 02

I assume every saved frame is an independent sample.

Starting question “Why does a huge trajectory still have uncertainty?”

A more useful question

Explain time correlation and effective sample size for an observable in an MD trajectory. Suggest how block analysis and independent runs can help, and avoid inventing a correlation time without data.

Why this helps: It asks for a property-specific uncertainty estimate supported by measurements.

Compare with an example answer

Nearby frames often retain memory of each other. Consequently, the number of independent observations can be much smaller than the number saved. Correlation analysis and suitably chosen blocks can help estimate uncertainty, with sufficient data and attention to stationarity. Saving more frequent frames does not by itself generate more independent information.

Course-authored teaching example. Your AI response may differ.

Follow-up to ask

How would I check that increasing a block length has reached a useful uncertainty plateau?

Check your understanding

Does doubling the number of saved frames by halving the save interval necessarily double independent information?

Reveal the check answer

No. Closely spaced frames may be strongly correlated.

Sticking point 03

I want to turn a histogram into free energy.

Starting question “Can I always take minus log of my histogram?”

A more useful question

Explain F(s) = -k_B*T*ln P(s) + C for a specified coordinate and probability measure. Discuss sampling, zero-count bins, and why radial distributions may require a geometric measure correction.

Why this helps: It supplies the coordinate issue and asks about the assumptions behind a transformation.

Compare with an example answer

A free-energy profile is related to the equilibrium probability density under a defined measure. Finite sampling, bin choice, and correlations affect its estimate. A zero-count bin indicates missing observed samples, not a measured infinite barrier. Radial coordinates include geometric volume factors, so a radial histogram and a radial distribution function must be distinguished.

Course-authored teaching example. Your AI response may differ.

Follow-up to ask

Which probability density does my histogram estimate, and does it include a radial volume factor?

Check your understanding

Does an empty histogram bin establish an infinite physical free-energy barrier?

Reveal the check answer

No. It may simply be unsampled.
